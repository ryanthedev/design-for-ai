Field Notes from the Print Shop

What a Ludlow caster teaches you about spacing

The machine will not let you cheat: every slug is exactly as wide as the matrices you locked into the stick, and the proof shows every lazy decision.

The first week on the Ludlow, I set a wedding invitation in 24-point Garamond and pulled a proof that looked like a picket fence. My teacher ran a thumbnail down the line and marked three joints where the brass spaces sat proud of the shoulder. Fix the metal, she said, and the page fixes itself.

A Ludlow slug cools in about forty seconds; you learn to plan your corrections while the pot reheats.

That habit, planning the correction before touching the machine, turned out to matter more than any single setting. Type is patient. It waits in the case until you have a reason to pick it up, and it holds whatever decision you make for as long as the form stays locked.

The stick does not forgive

Composing sticks measure in picas, and a pica is not a suggestion. When an apprentice crowds a line, the lockup springs the moment the quoins tighten. You hear it: a small metallic sigh, and the whole afternoon of work pancakes onto the stone.

Why the proof press still wins

You can preview a page on a screen for hours and never see what one pull of the proof press shows in a breath: the ink squash at the edges of the counters, the wobble in a worn matrix, the visible difference between a copper thin space and paper packing. The press reports what is actually there.

Set in Charter over a long winter. Printed on the shop's 1962 Vandercook, four pulls to a page.
